# Geer's framework — the pedagogical backbone

E. Harold Geer, *Organ Registration in Theory and Practice* (J. Fischer &
Bro., 1957) is the spine of this skill. Geer's contribution was to make
registration a *reasoned* discipline rather than a table of recipes: he starts
from acoustics, derives what the ear can and cannot resolve, and only then
turns to colour, mood, and historical practice. What follows is that
five-part structure **in our own words** with the operating principles we draw
from it — not a reproduction of the book (it is in copyright). Read it for the
*order of reasoning*: you cannot colour a line the ear cannot hear or that will
not blend.

## Part I — Scientific & technical (acoustics; the organ as a source of sound)

- A pipe's timbre is its harmonic spectrum. **Flue** pipes (principals,
  flutes, strings) speak by an air-sheet striking a lip; **reed** pipes speak
  by a beating tongue and are harmonically richer and more pungent.
- Pitch is named by **footage** — the length of the 8′ pipe that sounds at
  written pitch. 16′ sounds an octave below the key, 4′ an octave above, 2′
  two octaves above; fractional lengths (2⅔′, 1⅗′) sound harmonics (a fifth, a
  third) above the unison.
- A drawn stop is a *rank*: one pipe per key. Drawing several ranks stacks
  their spectra — this is how you build a composite tone the ear reads as one
  richer instrument.

## Part II — Application to tone combination (audibility, blend, production)

This is the part that governs Rule Priority 1–2. Geer's questions:

- **Audibility.** Will each intended line actually be heard? A stop can be
  masked by a louder neighbour, buried in the same register, or lost in the
  room. Prefer registrations that keep independent lines in distinct
  registers/colours.
- **Optimum blend.** Ranks combine well when the added rank reinforces the
  chorus without protruding: build a **pyramid** — the 8′ foundation loudest,
  each higher rank progressively softer, mixtures softest. A rank that sticks
  out is either a mistake or a deliberate *solo*.
- **Production/perception/propagation.** How the tone starts (speech/chiff),
  is perceived (fusion of partials into one colour), and travels (room,
  reverberation). Attack transients and a resonant space are part of the
  colour, not incidental.

## Part III — Application to interpretation (colour, mood, design)

- **Colour & mood.** Match the stop *family* to the affect: principals =
  firm/noble/liturgical; flutes = gentle/pastoral; strings = warm/keen; reeds
  = brilliant or plaintive. The music's character chooses the family.
- **Design.** A registration is a plan over the whole piece, not a moment. Give
  each section one clear intent.
- **Sequential design (terracing).** Baroque and earlier music is registered in
  **terraces** — discrete levels set per section (echo, *plein jeu* vs *récit*,
  full plenum vs a solo) — not a continuous swell. Decide *where* colour and
  weight step up or down, and why.

## Part IV — Media of performance (historical survey to 1750, and after)

The idiom sets the palette. Geer separates:

- **Latin countries (Italy/France/Spain) to 1750.** Registration by named
  combinations: French *plein jeu* (principal chorus + mixtures), *grand jeu*
  (reeds + cornet), *récit*/*tierce en taille* solos; the Italian *ripieno*.
- **Northern countries (Germany/Netherlands) to 1750 — the Werkprinzip.** The
  organ is several complete divisions (Hauptwerk/Great, Rückpositiv/Positiv,
  Brustwerk, Pedal), each a self-contained chorus with its own case and
  character; the **Pedal is independent and powerful** (Principal 16′, Posaune
  16′). This is the world of Buxtehude and Bach: *Organo Pleno* = the principal
  chorus of a division crowned by mixtures, pedal on 16′ + reed. Terraced
  contrast comes from *changing manuals* (Great ↔ Positiv), not from a swell box.
- **Later developments.** Romantic/orchestral registration adds enclosed
  (swell) divisions, orchestral colour stops, and continuous crescendo — a
  different aesthetic; use it only for that repertoire.

## Part V — Practical suggestions (planning; mechanical matters)

- **Plan on paper first**, section by section (the Output Pattern table): what
  each section should sound like, then the stops that get you there, then the
  changes between sections.
- **Mechanical realities** on a real organ: which hand/foot is free to change
  stops, pistons/combinations, coupler logic. In this renderer the analogue is
  *where you can place program-change / rank on-off events* (any tick — you are
  not limited by a player's free hand), and keeping the channel/program layout
  simple enough to reason about.

## How the parts map to Rule Priority

Pt II (audibility, blend) → priorities 1–2. Pt I / Pt III divisions & pedal →
priority 3. Pt III sequential design → priority 4. Pt III colour/mood →
priority 5. Pt IV idiom → priority 6. The renderer's clipping ceiling is our
added priority 7 — the physical stand-in for "the room can only take so much."
