# Time as dynamics — the model

## Why this exists

A violinist shapes a phrase with bow pressure; a singer with breath. An organist
has neither. The pipe speaks at the pressure the wind gives it, the plectrum
plucks with the force the jack gives it. Volume is **not available** as an
expressive parameter (the swell box is a late, partial, and non-Baroque
exception; and drawing stops changes *color and weight*, not phrase shape).

Everything expressive must therefore be carried by **when** notes start, **how
long** they sound, and **how much silence** lies between them.

## The core equivalence: separation ≈ softening

Perceptually the ear trades **density** for **loudness**. A line with air in it
reads as lighter and further away; a line whose notes touch reads as weightier
and closer. That gives a direct substitution table:

| Wanted | Realized as |
|---|---|
| *p* / lighter | shorter sounding-fraction, more silence between notes |
| *f* / weightier | fuller values, minimal gap, forward motion |
| *diminuendo* | progressively more separation, and/or broadening |
| *crescendo* | progressively more connection, and/or pressing forward |
| *sforzando* | **agogic accent** — lengthen the note itself |
| *tenuto* | hold the full written value |

The two physical levers differ by instrument:

- **Sustained (organ)** — the **release** is the lever. A note's *sounding
  fraction* of its written value is the whole game. (The attack is fixed by the
  pipe's speech; see [[organ-pipe-physics]].)
- **Plucked (harpsichord, lute)** — the **space between attacks** is the lever,
  plus **chord spread**: arpeggiating a chord, and how widely, is the
  harpsichord's accent. A spread chord *is* an emphasized chord.

## The metric hierarchy (good and bad notes)

Baroque theory ranks the beats: *notes bonnes* / *notes mauvaises*, "good" and
"bad" notes. In 4/4 the hierarchy is **1 > 3 > 2 = 4**, and within a beat the
on-beat subdivision outranks the off-beat. In compound meters (6/8, 12/8) the
dotted-beat heads outrank everything between them.

Strong beats are given **more time**; weak ones less. This is *agogic accent* —
the accent made by duration rather than volume — and it is how the meter becomes
*audible* on an instrument that cannot stress by loudness. Applied gently and
mean-preserving (so the bar keeps its length), it makes the music breathe; applied
heavily it sounds seasick.

A dissonance, a suspension, or a phrase peak is a "good note" regardless of where
it falls, and takes the same treatment — extra length, resolving into a lighter
consonance.

## Punctuation: rubato as the diminuendo

Baroque music is **speech**, and speech has commas and full stops:

- **Cadences broaden.** Energy relaxes into a close; the broadening *is* the
  diminuendo. Final cadences broaden most, sectional cadences less, internal
  half-cadences least.
- **Phrase-ends lift.** A small separation before the next phrase begins is the
  breath between sentences.
- **Motion resumes after.** Coming out of a cadence, returning to tempo is the
  crescendo.

Frescobaldi says this outright in his toccata prefaces: the tempo should be "now
languid, now quick," held back at phrase-ends, not held to a metronomic beat.

## What this is not

- **Not rubato in the Romantic sense** — not a melody floating free over a steady
  accompaniment. Baroque rubato moves the *whole texture*.
- **Not swing / notes inégales everywhere.** Inequality is a French convention
  applied to specific note-values in specific idioms — not a global groove.
- **Not an effect.** If a listener notices "that was a nice ritardando," it was
  too much. The shaping should be felt as *meaning*, not observed as *gesture*.

## The commonest failure

**Playing too fast.** A fast tempo leaves no room for the articulation, agogics
and punctuation that carry all the expression on these instruments — so the
result is mechanical *precisely because* nothing has space to speak. When a render
sounds dead, suspect the tempo first, the touch second, and only then the meter.
