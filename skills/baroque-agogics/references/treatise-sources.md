# The sources — what the treatises actually say

The time-as-dynamics model is not a modern invention; the period sources say it
directly, because they were writing for players of exactly these fixed-dynamic
instruments. This is the grounding, in rough chronological order.

## Frescobaldi (1615/1637) — the toccata prefaces

Frescobaldi prefaces his toccata books with performance instructions that read
like a manifesto for flexible time: the music is **not to be played to a steady
beat**; the tempo should be "now languid, now quick," according to the affect;
**phrase-ends and cadences are to be held back** ("sostenendosi"); passages of
running notes may be pressed forward. He also tells the player that sections may
be ended early or taken separately — the piece is rhetorical, not metronomic.

*Use for:* toccatas, preludes, fantasias — anywhere improvisatory. This is the
license for substantial tempo flexibility between sections.

## François Couperin, *L'art de toucher le clavecin* (1716)

Couperin is explicit that the harpsichord's expression is in touch and time. Key
ideas: **legato and *suspension*** (delaying a note slightly for expressive
effect); **arpégement** (spreading chords — the harpsichord's accent and its way
of sustaining); and the French **notes inégales** convention (stepwise notes of
certain values played long-short). He complains that the harpsichord's inability
to swell must be compensated by these means.

*Use for:* French repertoire; the plucked-instrument levers (spread, inequality).

## Quantz, *Versuch einer Anweisung die Flöte traversiere zu spielen* (1752)

Wide-ranging on **tempo and good taste**: tempo chosen by the affect and by the
shortest note-values present (they must remain articulate — the direct source for
"if the fast notes can't speak, the tempo is wrong"). Describes the **ordinary
articulation** — notes normally *not* held their full value — and treats
inequality and the hierarchy of strong/weak notes.

*Use for:* choosing tempo; justifying the default non-legato touch.

## C.P.E. Bach, *Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen* (1753)

The central source, and the closest to the family's own practice. On **touch**:
notes are neither detached nor slurred by default but held roughly *half* their
value in ordinary playing (his oft-cited default), with a spectrum from
`staccato` to `legato` chosen by affect and tempo. On **agogics**: notes that are
to be *stressed* may be **held longer** — expression by duration where a
clavichord's *Bebung* or a fortepiano's dynamics are unavailable. Also the
authority behind our ornament realization (see the `realize()` engine).

*Use for:* the default articulation; the licence for agogic stress; ornaments.

## Türk, *Klavierschule* (1789)

The most **explicit and quantitative** on articulation: enumerates degrees of note
length — from very short (a quarter of the value) through ordinary (about half to
three-quarters) to fully held and over-held — and ties them to character and
tempo. Also discusses lengthening notes for emphasis and holding back at closes.

*Use for:* concrete numbers when choosing a sounding-fraction / gap.

## Harnoncourt, *Musik als Klangrede* (1982)

The modern synthesis, and where the framing of this skill comes from: Baroque
music is **speech** ("sounding speech"), governed by rhetoric — phrases as
sentences, articulation as pronunciation, the strong/weak hierarchy as
prosody. His polemic: playing this music with a modern, even, legato,
metronomic sound strips out precisely the expressive layer the composers relied
on. He is also the clearest voice on **the meter must be felt, not counted**.

*Use for:* the overall stance, and for deciding *why* a passage is shaped.

## How to use them together

- **Tempo** — Quantz (shortest notes must speak), Frescobaldi (flexible by
  section/affect).
- **Touch** — C.P.E. Bach (ordinary ≈ half value), Türk (degrees), Couperin
  (French legato/suspension).
- **Agogic stress** — C.P.E. Bach (stressed notes held longer), Harnoncourt
  (prosody).
- **Punctuation** — Frescobaldi (hold back at cadences), Harnoncourt (rhetoric).

A caution: these sources describe *different* national styles and decades, and
they disagree at the edges (the French inequality conventions are not German
practice). Match the source to the repertoire rather than blending them into one
average style.
